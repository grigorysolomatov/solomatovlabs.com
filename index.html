Error-correcting codes

A linear code is a subspace of length-n vectors over a finite field. A k-row generator matrix G turns a message m into the codeword mG, adding redundancy so that errors on the channel can be found and corrected. A parity-check matrix H tests a received word r: it is a codeword exactly when HrT = 0, and a nonzero syndrome HrT points to the error.

The binary Hamming [7,4] code: 4 message bits, 3 parity bits. The columns of H are the seven nonzero 3-bit vectors, so one flipped bit gives a syndrome equal to its column, which locates and corrects it.

Codewords are evaluations of low-degree polynomials. Two codewords agree in fewer than k places, so up to (n−k)/2 errors can be corrected. Here G evaluates 1 and x at the points 1, 2, 3, 4 of the field with 5 elements, and H gives the two parity sums (mod 5) every codeword satisfies. Used in QR codes, CDs and deep-space links.

Smallest example: q = 2. The Hermitian curve has 8 points over the field with 4 elements {0, 1, α, α2}. Each row of G evaluates one function (1, X, Y, X2) at these points, giving an [8,4,4] code. Reed–Solomon codes over the same field reach length 5 at most. This code is its own dual (GGT = 0), so G is also its parity-check matrix.
